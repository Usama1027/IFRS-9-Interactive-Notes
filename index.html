CONCEPT 01 · FOUNDATIONS

What is IFRS 9 — and why?

IFRS 9 sets the rules for recognising, classifying, measuring and impairing financial instruments and for hedge accounting. It replaced the rule-heavy, complex IAS 39.

REAL-LIFE EXAMPLE

During the 2008 financial crisis, banks booked loan losses "too little, too late" because IAS 39 only recognised losses after a loss event occurred. The G20 asked standard-setters for a fix — IFRS 9's forward-looking expected-loss model is the answer.

Start with the 'why'. IAS 39 was criticised as complex and, in the 2008 crisis, for delaying loss recognition. IFRS 9 was finalised in 2014 and became mandatory for annual periods beginning on or after 1 January 2018. Walk through the three pillars; each gets its own section. CONCEPT 02 · FOUNDATIONS

Recognition & Initial Measurement

An entity recognises a financial asset or liability when it becomes party to the contractual provisions of the instrument.

⚖️

Initial measurement

At fair value, plus transaction costs — unless the item is measured at FVTPL (then costs go straight to profit or loss).

🧾

Trade receivables

Without a significant financing component, measured at the transaction price (IFRS 15).

📅

Day-one gain / loss

Recognised only if fair value is evidenced by an observable market price or observable-only inputs. Otherwise deferred.

REAL-LIFE EXAMPLE

A company buys corporate bonds for $1,000,000 and pays $5,000 in broker fees.

Amortised cost
$1,005,000
FVTPL
$1,000,000 + $5,000 expense

🔑 Key rule

Transaction costs are capitalised for AC & FVOCI items, but expensed immediately for FVTPL items.

Recognition happens when the entity becomes party to the contract – for example, when you sign a loan agreement or buy a bond. Initial measurement is fair value, adjusted for transaction costs unless the instrument goes through profit or loss. The bond example shows the same purchase producing different carrying amounts depending on classification.

CONCEPT 04 · CLASSIFICATION

The Business Model Test

Assessed at portfolio level — based on how the business is actually managed and how performance is reported, not on management's intention for a single asset.

🏦

Hold to Collect

Objective: collect contractual cash flows. Sales are infrequent or insignificant, or close to maturity, or due to rising credit risk.

→ Amortised Cost

💧

Hold to Collect & Sell

Both collecting cash flows and selling are integral — typical for liquidity management portfolios.

→ FVOCI

📈

Other (Trading)

Held for trading, managed on a fair value basis, or collecting cash flows is incidental.

→ FVTPL

REAL-LIFE EXAMPLES
  • Retail bank making 20-year mortgages to earn interest → hold to collect.
  • Insurer holding government bonds, selling some to pay claims → collect & sell.
  • Investment bank trading desk buying bonds to profit from price moves → other / trading.
The business model is a matter of fact, evidenced by how the portfolio is managed, how performance is evaluated, and the frequency and reason for past sales. It is not a free choice instrument by instrument. Sales don't automatically disqualify hold-to-collect, but frequent, significant sales may.
CONCEPT 05 · CLASSIFICATION

The SPPI Test

Solely Payments of Principal and Interest on the principal outstanding. Interest = basic lending return, nothing more exotic.

What interest may include

  • Time value of money
  • Credit risk
  • Other basic lending risks (e.g. liquidity) & costs
  • A profit margin
✅

Passes

  • Plain fixed-rate loan
  • Floating-rate loan (e.g. SOFR + margin)
  • Ordinary government bond
❌

Fails

  • Convertible bond (equity upside)
  • Returns linked to an equity index or commodity price
  • Leveraged or inverse-floater coupons
REAL-LIFE EXAMPLE

A bank lends $1m at fixed 6% → SPPI passes. An investor buys a bond convertible into the issuer's shares → returns depend on share price, not just lending risk → SPPI fails → the whole instrument is measured at FVTPL.

SPPI asks: are the cash flows basic lending flows? If there is leverage, equity or commodity exposure, the answer is no. Note that for financial assets, embedded derivatives are not separated – the whole hybrid asset is tested against SPPI, which is why the convertible bond ends up wholly at FVTPL.
CONCEPT 06 · CLASSIFICATION

Equity Investments & the Fair Value Option

Equity instruments never pass SPPI, so the default is FVTPL — but there is an irrevocable election for strategic holdings.

🏷️

FVOCI election for equity

  • Made at initial recognition, per instrument, irrevocable
  • Not available if held for trading
  • Fair value changes → OCI, never recycled to P&L
  • Dividends → P&L; no impairment testing
🎛️

Fair Value Option (FVO)

  • Designate an item at FVTPL at initial recognition
  • Allowed only if it removes or reduces an accounting mismatch
  • Irrevocable
REAL-LIFE EXAMPLE

A manufacturer holds an 8% strategic stake in a key supplier — not to trade. It elects FVOCI. When the share price falls, the loss sits in OCI and doesn't hit profit; when the shares are sold the gain/loss is not moved to P&L.

Equity investments are FVTPL by default. For non-trading equity, entities may elect FVOCI at initial recognition. That election is irrevocable and means no recycling – so there's no impairment and no gain or loss ever reaches profit, except dividends. The fair value option is a separate tool to fix accounting mismatches.
CONCEPT 07 · CLASSIFICATION

Classifying Financial Liabilities

Mostly unchanged from IAS 39 — with one important twist: own credit risk.

📄

Default: Amortised cost

Most liabilities — loans, bonds issued, payables — are measured at amortised cost using the effective interest method.

⚡

FVTPL liabilities

  • Held for trading (incl. derivatives)
  • Designated under the Fair Value Option

🔄 The own-credit twist

For FVO liabilities, the part of fair value change caused by the entity's own credit risk goes to OCI, not profit — unless that creates an accounting mismatch. It's not recycled. Liabilities are never reclassified.

REAL-LIFE EXAMPLE

A company's bond is at FVO. Its credit rating drops, so the bond's fair value falls. Under IAS 39 this showed as a gain in profit (odd!). Under IFRS 9 that gain goes to OCI.

Liability accounting is largely carried over. The key change is own-credit: under IAS 39, a deteriorating credit rating produced a gain in profit on fair-valued debt, which confused users. IFRS 9 puts that component in OCI. Also note that liabilities are never reclassified after initial recognition.
CONCEPT 08 · MEASUREMENT

Subsequent Measurement

Where do the numbers go? It depends on the category. Interest revenue uses the effective interest rate (EIR) — the rate that discounts estimated future cash flows to the gross carrying amount.

AMORTISED COST
  • Interest (EIR) → P&L
  • ECL impairment → P&L
  • Fair value changes → ignored
FVOCI (DEBT)
  • Interest (EIR) → P&L
  • ECL impairment → P&L
  • Fair value changes → OCI, recycled to P&L on sale
FVTPL
  • All fair value changes → P&L
  • No separate impairment
  • Transaction costs expensed
REAL-LIFE EXAMPLE

A bank lends $100,000 at a 5% EIR. Year-1 interest revenue = $100,000 × 5% = $5,000 recognised in P&L. If the market value of the loan later falls, nothing changes in the books (amortised cost) — only an ECL allowance would.

This slide ties the earlier tests to the accounting entries. Amortised cost: interest and impairment in P&L. FVOCI debt: same P&L items, but fair value movements in OCI with recycling on derecognition – a key contrast with FVOCI equity, which doesn't recycle. FVTPL: everything in profit or loss.
CONCEPT 09 · IMPAIRMENT

The 3-Stage Expected Credit Loss Model

No need to wait for a loss event. Provisions are recognised from day one, and rise as credit quality deteriorates. Click a stage.

Click through the stages. Stage 1: no significant increase in credit risk – 12-month ECL. Stage 2: significant increase in credit risk since initial recognition – lifetime ECL. Stage 3: credit-impaired or defaulted – lifetime ECL and interest revenue switches to the net carrying amount. 30 days past due is a rebuttable presumption of significant increase in credit risk; 90 days is a rebuttable presumption of default. Purchased or originated credit-impaired assets are a special case with lifetime ECL from the start.

CONCEPT 11 · HEDGE ACCOUNTING

Hedge Accounting

Normally, a derivative at fair value creates P&L volatility that the hedged item doesn't. Hedge accounting matches the timing of gains and losses — and is optional.

🏷️

Fair value hedge

Hedges changes in fair value of an asset, liability or firm commitment. Both hedge and hedged item adjust through P&L.

🌊

Cash flow hedge

Hedges variability of future cash flows. Effective part held in OCI (cash flow hedge reserve), moved to P&L when the hedged item affects it.

🌍

Net investment hedge

Hedges FX risk of a foreign operation. Effective part goes to OCI (translation reserve) until disposal.

✅ Qualifying criteria (principles, not rules)

  • Formal documentation of relationship & risk objective
  • Economic relationship between item and hedge
  • Credit risk doesn't dominate value changes
  • Hedge ratio reflects actual risk management; can be rebalanced

The old 80–125% "bright line" test is gone.

REAL-LIFE EXAMPLE

An airline expects to buy jet fuel in 6 months and locks the price with fuel futures (cash flow hedge). Futures gains/losses wait in OCI and move into P&L when the fuel is consumed — so reported fuel cost reflects the hedged price.

Hedge accounting is an accounting election that aligns reporting with risk management. Three models: fair value, cash flow and net investment hedges. IFRS 9 replaced the 80–125% effectiveness bright line with a principles-based approach: economic relationship, credit risk not dominating, and a hedge ratio consistent with actual risk management. Entities may continue to apply IAS 39 hedge accounting as a policy choice for now, and macro hedging has separate guidance.
CONCEPT 12 · LIFECYCLE

Reclassification & Derecognition

What happens when things change — or when an instrument leaves the balance sheet.

🔁

Reclassification

  • Financial assets: only when the business model changes (rare)
  • Applied prospectively from the reclassification date (first day of the next reporting period)
  • Financial liabilities: never reclassified
🚪

Derecognition

  • Asset: rights to cash flows expire, or are transferred with substantially all risks & rewards
  • Liability: obligation is discharged, cancelled or expires
  • Substantial modification of liability terms (PV differs by ≥ 10%) = extinguishment
EXAMPLE · RECLASSIFICATION

A bank decides to wind down its lending business and sell a loan book it used to hold. The business model changed → loans move from amortised cost to FVTPL from the next reporting period.

EXAMPLE · DERECOGNITION

A company factors receivables. Without recourse (buyer takes the credit risk) → derecognise. With recourse (company keeps the risk) → receivables stay on the balance sheet with a matching borrowing.

Reclassification of assets is rare and triggered only by a change in business model, applied prospectively with no restatement. Liabilities can't be reclassified. For derecognition, think in terms of contractual rights and the transfer of risks and rewards. The factoring example is a classic exam scenario – with or without recourse changes the answer.

Key Takeaways

Six ideas to remember when the lights come back on.

1️⃣

Two tests

Business model + SPPI decide AC, FVOCI or FVTPL.

2️⃣

Equity is special

FVTPL by default; irrevocable FVOCI election with no recycling.

3️⃣

Liabilities

Mostly amortised cost; own credit on FVO items goes to OCI.

4️⃣

ECL, 3 stages

12-month ECL → lifetime ECL → lifetime ECL on credit-impaired assets.

Hedge accounting

Principle-based; fair value, cash flow and net investment hedges.

Lifecycle

Reclassify only on business-model change; derecognise on transfer of risks & rewards.
